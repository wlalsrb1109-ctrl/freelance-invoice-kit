Why “salary ÷ 2,080” gives you the wrong rate

The usual way freelancers set a rate is to take the last salary, divide by the 2,080 hours in a working year and add a bit. That number quietly guarantees you earn less than you did as an employee, because three things are missing from it:

  • Tax. A salary figure is what you took home after tax. Your invoice is what you earn before it. Gross-up the take-home you want instead.
  • Expenses. Laptop, software, insurance, coworking and an accountant used to come out of someone else's budget. Now they come out of your revenue.
  • Unbillable time. Nobody pays you to write proposals, chase invoices, do your books or find the next client. A 40-hour week is rarely a 40-hour billable week.

The formula this calculator uses

hourly rate = ((take-home ÷ (1 − tax rate)) + expenses) × (1 + buffer) ÷ billable hours per year

Everything else on the page is a conversion of that single number: a day rate is the hourly rate times the billable hours in a day, a week rate times the billable hours in a week, and a monthly retainer is the annual revenue divided by twelve. When you quote a project, the contingency percentage is what protects you from the estimate being wrong.

Reading your result

If the hourly number looks shockingly high next to what you charge today, check the billable-hours field first: at 25 billable hours a week, a $60,000 take-home with $6,000 of expenses and 25% tax needs roughly $86 an hour. That is not greed — it is arithmetic. And if the number looks low, you are probably assuming you can bill every hour you work.

This is a planning tool, not tax or financial advice. Tax systems vary by country and by how your business is set up; confirm your set-aside percentage with an accountant.
